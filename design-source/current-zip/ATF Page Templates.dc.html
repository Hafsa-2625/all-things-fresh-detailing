Detailing schedules written for California do not survive a Cleveland winter. Between lake-effect snow, the salt that follows it, and a heavy spring pollen season, a car in Northeast Ohio takes on more in six months than most cars take on in two years.

The short answer

Two full details a year, timed to the seasons rather than the calendar. One in late March or early April once the salt trucks stop running, and one in October before they start again. If the car lives outside year round, or carries kids, pets, or job-site dirt, add a lighter interior refresh every three months between those two.

A working schedule
Late March / early April Full detail, salt removal
Late May Exterior wash after pollen
July Interior refresh
October Full detail, protection applied

Why salt is the deciding factor

Road salt does not stay on the road. It comes into the cabin on your shoes, dries into the carpet fibres, and pulls moisture out of the air every time the car warms up. That is the white crust you see on floor mats in February, and it is why a vacuum alone does not fix it. Salt needs water and extraction to leave.

Outside, the same salt sits in the seams of rocker panels and wheel arches. On a car that is never washed between November and March, that is five months of continuous contact. A wash every two to three weeks through winter costs far less than the bodywork it prevents.

Signs you have waited too long

The carpet is stiff, not just dirty Dried salt has bonded to the fibres. Extraction, not vacuuming.
Windows fog and stay fogged Trapped moisture in the upholstery, usually with a film on the inside of the glass.
Paint feels rough to a bare hand Bonded contamination. It needs decontamination before any protection will hold.

If any of those describe your car right now, the honest answer is that the schedule matters less than getting it reset once. Book a full detail, then pick up the seasonal rhythm from there.
